Unfire research note · 21 November 2026

Who authorises an AI agent's next action?

An AI can suggest what to do next. That suggestion should not automatically become permission to act.

By Omar Baró · Unfire, an independent founder-led technology initiative in Catalonia, Spain · Not peer reviewed

A distinction that matters

Language-model output can be fluent, confident and wrong. When a model proposes changing files, releasing information or completing a workflow, the operational question is different from the conversational one: what independently decides whether the action is permitted?

Consider a document-review assistant that notices a discrepancy in a synthetic invoice. Its proposal could be useful, but the underlying authority to alter the record, disclose a document or approve a payment cannot be established by that proposal alone. In a real organisation, policies, authenticated approvals, record integrity and auditability also matter.

A policy gate is one way to separate suggestions from action authority. A restricted, typed proposal can be evaluated against rules that are not supplied by the untrusted model response.

A small public experiment, not a commercial deployment

Unfire's BOLT Agent Control Plane is a separate MIT-licensed research demonstration. The current public repository applies local, deterministic rules to structured proposed actions and produces one of three classifications:

Allow: an eligible action type passes the local rule set.

Review required: a proposal is flagged as requiring separate human attention.

Deny: a proposal is disallowed by the local rule set.

These labels are read-only classifications. The experiment does not execute proposed actions, authenticate an approver, integrate with a customer's enterprise systems or enforce real-world permissions. It should not be confused with the separately distributed commercial Bolt macOS application or with Unfire Autonomy Convergence and Unfire Control Vision research.

A fixed research pre-release was published on 21 November 2026. The associated technical brief records 12 locally passing offline checks on a specified public commit. These are narrow software checks, not security certification, a formal audit or evidence of production reliability.

What a serious evaluation would still require

  • Independent policy ownership: decide who can create and modify rules, rather than allowing model output to redefine them.
  • Trusted identity and approval: bind review decisions to authenticated people, roles and an explicit action scope.
  • Careful boundary testing: exercise malformed proposals, prompt injection, retries, race conditions and conflicting instructions.
  • Reconstructible evidence: record what was proposed, the policy version used, the decision reached and any authorised downstream execution.
  • Real-world validation before claims: measure limitations, operational risks and acceptance criteria with a prospective customer under agreed legal, technical and privacy conditions.

None of these higher-assurance capabilities is claimed to have been established by the public read-only experiment. They identify the gap between a useful demonstration and a deployable service.
